What is percent recovery in method validation?

In analytical chemistry, percent recovery measures what fraction of the analyte present in a sample is actually detected and quantified by the method. It is the usual way to assess trueness: you analyze a sample whose content is known (a certified reference material, a standard or a spiked sample) and compare the result with the expected value. A recovery of 100 % means the method introduces no bias: what is in the sample is exactly what you measure.

This is not the same as the percent recovery of a recrystallization or synthesis, where you compare the mass of product obtained with the mass you started with.

Formulas

% Recovery = (measured value / nominal value) × 100

Absolute error = measured value − nominal value

Relative error (%) = (absolute error / nominal value) × 100

Bias (%) = mean % recovery − 100

The summary also reports the standard deviation of the recoveries and the coefficient of variation (CV%), which in pharmaceutical work is usually called the relative standard deviation (%RSD). Recovery tells you how close you are to the true value; CV% tells you how repeatable that recovery is.

Typical acceptance criteria

Common ranges for mean recovery and precision

80 – 120 % → routine methods (water, food, industrial samples)

90 – 110 % → precision methods (pharmaceutical, accredited methods)

95 – 105 % → high-accuracy methods (pharmacopeial, reference methods)

CV% < 5 % → acceptable repeatability in most cases

These are typical values, not universal limits. The criteria that apply to your method come from your validation plan and from the guideline or standard it follows (for example AOAC, ICH Q2 or a pharmacopeia), which may set different limits depending on the analyte, the matrix and the concentration level. Frequently asked questions

Can I use this calculator for spike recovery?

Yes, as long as you subtract the unspiked result first. Enter the amount of analyte added as the nominal value and the amount found (spiked result minus unspiked result) as the measured value. The calculator does not take the unspiked sample as a separate input, so that subtraction is up to you, in the same units.

How many samples do I need to evaluate recovery?

EURACHEM recommends at least 6 independent determinations at each concentration level. For a full validation, at least 3 concentration levels are evaluated (low, medium and high within the working range). ICH Q2 sets a minimum of 9 determinations over at least 3 levels for accuracy.

Does a low recovery mean the method is bad?

Not necessarily. A consistently low recovery (e.g. 85 %) may be acceptable if it is constant and known; in that case a correction factor is applied. What matters is that the recovery is reproducible: a high CV% is a bigger problem than a recovery that is far from 100 % but stable.
