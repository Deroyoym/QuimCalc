What are the LOD and the LOQ?

        

          The limit of detection (LOD) is the lowest concentration of an analyte that can be distinguished from the background noise with a stated probability of error (typically 5 %). Below the LOD the instrument may "see something", but it cannot confirm that it is the analyte.
        

        

          The limit of quantitation (LOQ), also called limit of quantification, is the lowest concentration that can be measured with acceptable precision and accuracy for the purpose of the analysis. It is always higher than the LOD and sets the lower end of the method's working range.
        


        
Which method should I use?


        

          Blank method (ISO 11843 / IUPAC) — suitable for most cases
          
Measure the blank at least 10 times and calculate the mean and the standard deviation (s):

          
    
            
  • LOD = mean + 3 × s
    • 
            
  • LOQ = mean + 10 × s
    • 
          

          
It is experimental and does not depend on the calibration model. The readings must be expressed in concentration units; if you record signals, divide by the calibration slope first.

        


        

          Calibration curve method (ICH Q2) — the usual choice in pharmaceutical validation
          
    
            
  • LOD = 3.3 × Se / m
    • 
            
  • LOQ = 10 × Se / m
    • 
          

          
Where Se is the standard error of the regression (residual standard deviation) and m is the slope. This is the approach described in the ICH Q2 guideline and requires a calibration curve: fit it first and use its slope and standard error.

        


        

          Signal-to-noise method — for chromatographic and spectroscopic peaks
          
    
            
  • LOD: concentration giving a signal 3 times the baseline noise (S/N = 3)
    • 
            
  • LOQ: concentration giving S/N = 10
    • 
          

          
Widely used in HPLC, GC and atomic absorption when the baseline noise can be measured directly on the chromatogram or spectrum.

        


        
Frequently asked questions


        

          
Why is the LOQ always higher than the LOD?

          

            Because quantifying with acceptable precision needs more signal than simply detecting. The LOD says "something is there"; the LOQ says "we can measure it accurately enough for the result to be useful". In practice, a sample between the LOD and the LOQ is reported as "detected, not quantifiable" or simply as below the LOQ.
          

        


        

          
How often should the LOD and LOQ be verified?

          

            In laboratories accredited to ISO/IEC 17025, the limits are verified when the instrument changes, when a critical reagent lot changes, after major maintenance, or at the frequency set by the laboratory's validation procedure. Annual verification, or verification after any change that may affect method sensitivity, is common practice.
          

        


        

          
Can I use this calculator for accreditation or validation reports?

          

            The calculations follow the standard statistical methods (ISO 11843, ICH Q2). The step-by-step output can be attached to the laboratory's internal records. For formal validation or accreditation reports, the laboratory must verify the results under its own documented procedure and with the original instrument data.
